OWNERSHIP DISCIPLINE

The Lifelong Care of Specialty Cutlery

A master-forged knife is not a disposable consumable—it is a generational culinary instrument. With basic daily attentiveness, your blades will remain acute, beautiful, and functional for decades.

Preservation Protocols

The Seven Pillars of Knife Maintenance

Methodical daily practices to maintain razor apex geometry and handle integrity.

01

Hand Washing Only

NEVER DISHWASH

Dishwashers subject cutlery to caustic alkaline detergents, aggressive high-pressure water jets, and vibrating racks that knock edges against porcelain. This dulls acute micro-bevels instantly and bleaches the protective oils from hardwood handles.

Best Practice: Wash by hand immediately after prep with warm water, mild soap, and a non-abrasive cellulose sponge. Keep the sharp edge directed away from your hand.

02

Immediate Towel Drying

ZERO AIR-DRYING

Leaving fine cutlery in drying racks allows mineral-heavy water droplets to evaporate slowly on the blade face, causing pin-point pit corrosion even on stainless steels. On carbon steels, standing water creates rapid orange surface rust.

Best Practice: Wipe completely dry with a lint-free cotton kitchen towel, starting from the spine and drawing toward the cutting edge in one confident motion.

03

Proper Cutting Surfaces

SURFACE ABSORPTION

The surface beneath your ingredients determines 50% of your blade's edge longevity. Glass, marble, granite, ceramic, and bamboo are harder than steel. When an acute 15° apex strikes these materials, the steel microscopically rolls or chips.

Best Practice: Use end-grain hardwoods (Walnut, Cherry, Maple) or soft food-grade rubber boards (such as Asahi or Hasegawa) that yield gently to the knife tip.

04

Safe Storage Solutions

GUARD THE APEX

Tossing unprotected knives into kitchen drawers causes catastrophic metal-on-metal impact with spoons and ladles, blunting edges and endangering fingers reaching into the drawer.

Best Practice: Store knives on wood-faced magnetic strips, in horizontal slotted blocks, or inside felt-lined wooden sayas (blade sheaths).

05

Honing vs. Sharpening

REALIGNMENT VS REMOVAL

Honing does not remove steel—it realigns microscopic teeth that have deflected sideways during cutting. Use a ceramic honing rod weekly at a 15° angle with feather-light pressure (no heavier than the weight of the knife itself).

Best Practice: Use a 2,000-grit ceramic rod 4–5 strokes per side before every major prep session. Avoid coarse grooved metal butcher steels on hard Japanese blades.

06

Moisture & Acid Control

BOTANICAL OILS

Acidic ingredients like lemons, tomatoes, and shallots break down surface passivity. For carbon steel and Damascus blades, a wipe-down during prep is mandatory to prevent discolored pitting.

Best Practice: Apply a drop of 100% food-grade Japanese camellia oil (Tsubaki) to the blade face and handle monthly to nourish wood and create an impenetrable moisture barrier.

Critical Precautions

What to Avoid With Premium Culinary Cutlery

No Frozen Food Hacking: Never chop into frozen proteins or ice blocks; cold brittle steel will chip immediately.
No Bone Splitting: Chef knives and santokus are precision slicers, not butchery axes. Use thick cleavers for poultry bones.
No Lateral Board Scraping: Never scrape chopped ingredients across the board using the sharp edge; flip the knife and use the spine.
No Twisting Motions: When cutting dense squash, cut straight down. Twisting or prying causes micro-fractures along thin apexes.
No Soaking in Sinks: Never leave knives submerged under soapy dishwater where handles rot and hidden edges cut hands.
No Pull-Through Electric Wheels: Motorized dry wheels heat and destroy factory heat treatment permanently.
